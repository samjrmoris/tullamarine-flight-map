How loud, by distance from the runway

Landing planes descend on a fixed 3° slope, so their height depends only on the distance to touchdown. Being 1 to 2 km to the side of the path cuts the noise a lot, even close in.

Departures are louder because the engines run at take-off power, but they climb faster and spread onto different routes.

How the loudness is worked out

For each spot, the model finds how close each corridor passes in 3D and looks up the peak level heard on the ground, LAmax, from NATS representative aircraft noise tables (single-aisle 125–180 seat jets and 250-seat twin-aisle jets). It then applies the standard SAE AIR 1751 extra ground attenuation for aircraft seen low to the side. Wide-body levels are calibrated to Melbourne Airport's third runway fact sheet, which quotes 80 dB(A) for a wide-body arrival at 600 ft and 82 dB(A) for a departure at 1,300 ft. Newer 787 and A350 types are quieter close in than the NATS average. The typical-jet levels already match the fact sheet's 78 to 79 dB(A).

The map shows the loudest single overflight at each spot, not how many flights there are each day. The runways in use change with the wind, so a spot may only hear planes some of the time. Corridors are simplified: straight 3° approaches and generic departure fans. Real tracks spread out and departures follow set routes. Treat the numbers as a guide, accurate to within about ±5 dB.
